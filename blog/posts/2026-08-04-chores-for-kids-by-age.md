Every chore chart I've seen starts the same way. Laminated, colour-coded, stuck to the fridge with real ceremony. By the second Wednesday there's a sticker missing, by the third nobody's looking at it, and by half-term it's holding up a takeaway menu.

The chart isn't the problem. The problem is that most charts ask for too much, too vaguely, and then nobody checks.

## What each age can actually do

Kids can do more than we tend to give them, as long as the job is specific and the same every time. Here's roughly where we'd start:

:::checklist{label="A starting point, by age"}
- **4–6:** lay the table, put their own shoes and coat away, feed a pet with help
- **7–9:** clear their plate, pair socks, empty the cutlery drawer of the dishwasher, feed the pets
- **10–12:** put their own washing on and away, take the bins out, make a simple packed lunch
- **13+:** cook one simple dinner a week, change their own bed, do a supermarket list
:::

That's a starting point, not a target. A confident six-year-old might empty the whole dishwasher. A nervous twelve-year-old might not be ready to use the hob. Pick from the row that fits, not the row that matches their birthday.

## Two jobs, not ten

The chart that lasts is short. Two jobs each is enough to begin with. A long list turns every job into a negotiation ("I did the table, so I shouldn't have to do the bins"). A short one just gets done.

Add one job a term, not one a week. You're building a habit, and habits need boring repetition more than variety.

## Same job, same day

"Help out more" isn't a chore. "Bins out on Thursday night" is. When the job and the day never change, nobody has to remember whose turn it is, and that removes most of the arguing.

If you rotate jobs between kids, rotate monthly, not daily. Daily rotation means someone always needs to check the chart, and the chart is exactly the thing that gets ignored.

:::quote
A chore chart doesn't fail because the kids stop caring. It fails because the grown-ups stop checking.
:::

## Check it, every time

This is the unglamorous bit. For the first few weeks, a grown-up has to notice every single time a job gets done, or doesn't. Not with a lecture, just a look at the chart and a "bins done, nice". Once the job is a habit you can check less. Stop checking before that and the chart quietly dies.

Pocket money helps some families and not others. If you use it, tie it to the whole week rather than individual jobs, so it doesn't turn every task into a price negotiation.

## When the paper runs out

Once kids are old enough to have a phone, the chart can live there instead. In [Noa](https://www.asknoa.app/features/tasks) you can give each child a repeating task ("bins, every Thursday") with a reminder, and tick it off from your phone when you spot it done. That's the same chart, with one less thing stuck to the fridge.
