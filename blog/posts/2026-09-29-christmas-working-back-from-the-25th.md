It's a Saturday in the middle of December and the queue at the Post Office is out of the door and past the bins. Somewhere in it is a parent holding three padded envelopes for cousins in Aberdeen, phone open on the Royal Mail page, doing the maths on whether first class will still make it.

Almost nothing about Christmas is actually difficult. The problem is that twenty easy jobs all come due in the same fortnight, and about half of them quietly had a deadline in November.

So here is the countdown I'd run, working backwards from the 25th. Christmas Day is a Friday this year, which means the last useful working week starts on Monday 21 December, and in most of the country the schools will have broken up by then. Anything that needs a shop, a post office or another adult's agreement has to land before that week, not in it.

:::checklist{label="Six weeks, one job each"}
- Week of 16 Nov: decide who's hosting, who's travelling, what you're spending
- Week of 23 Nov: put every school date in the calendar, with a name against it
- Week of 30 Nov: look up the last posting dates and buy the things that have to travel
- Week of 7 Dec: book the food — order, slot, collection time
- Week of 14 Dec: wrap, post the rest, survive the last week of term
- Week of 21 Dec: nothing new goes in the diary
:::

## The third week of November is the one that matters

Three decisions, made once, out loud, while everyone is still calm: who's hosting Christmas Day, who's doing Boxing Day, and roughly what you're spending on presents.

None of these get easier by being left. They get harder, because by the first week of December your mother-in-law has made an assumption, your sister has booked a train, and the conversation you didn't have in November now has feelings attached to it. One message to the wider family in mid-November — "we're at ours this year, come from twelve" — is worth more than every clever planner in the app store.

:::quote
Christmas doesn't go wrong in December. It goes wrong in the third week of November, when nobody quite decides who's hosting.
:::

While you're there, write the gift list once. One line per person, one number next to it. The list that ruins December is the one that lives in three heads and gets re-invented every time someone walks past a shop.

## School will ambush you, and it always does it twice

Nativity, carol service, Christmas jumper day, class party, the thing where you have to send in a decorated shoebox. These arrive in dribs and drabs, in book bags, on a letter that's already two days old.

Take twenty minutes in the week of 23 November and put every date you have into the calendar in one sitting, each one with a person's name on it. Not "nativity, 10am" — "nativity, 10am, Dad's going". The dates are only half the information; the other half is which adult is standing in a cold hall. If you want the full version of this, I wrote about [school admin without the 8.40am panic](/blog/school-admin-for-parents) earlier in the year, and December is just that month with tinsel on.

The ones that get missed are the "it's only ten minutes" ones: the singing at the garden centre, the last-day party where you have to collect at noon. Ten minutes at eleven in the morning still costs someone half a day off work.

## Post before you shop

This is the bit that catches everyone. If a present has to go in the post, your shopping deadline isn't Christmas Eve — it's Royal Mail's, and second class is earlier than first class by a few days. They publish the dates each autumn, so look them up once, in the week of 30 November, and put both dates in the calendar as all-day events with a reminder two days before.

Then work backwards from there. Anything travelling has to be bought, wrapped and addressed by that morning, which in practice means the last weekend in November or the first in December. Cards abroad are earlier still.

While you're in the calendar, set that reminder to repeat every year. The dates shift a day or two, but the reminder to go and check them doesn't need to be re-invented every November.

## Order food, don't buy food

The week of 7 December is for booking, not carrying. The turkey or the nut roast, the veg order, the delivery slot for the big shop, the collection time at the butcher. Delivery slots for the week of the 21st go long before the week of the 21st.

Put the collection time itself in the calendar, not just "food ordered". A slot at 9am on the 23rd is an appointment, and it belongs next to everything else that morning so you can see it clashes with the haircut before you book the haircut.

## The last week is closed

From Monday 21 December, the rule is that nothing new goes in. No new baking project, no new craft, no new tradition someone saw on Instagram. You do the things you already committed to, in the order you committed to them, and the gaps stay gaps.

Three things I'd cut without a second thought: the advent calendar with twenty-four different activities in it, matching wrapping paper, and anything handmade for teachers. A card the child wrote themselves is fine. It has always been fine.
