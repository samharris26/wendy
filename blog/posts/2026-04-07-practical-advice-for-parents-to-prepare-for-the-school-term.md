## The Same Crisis, Every Term

Every parent knows the pattern. The holiday is coming to an end. The children are fractious and over-stimulated and have forgotten what a bedtime is. The uniform is somewhere, probably. The PE kit was definitely washed at some point. There's a letter from school that arrived before half-term and may or may not contain important information about the first day back.

And on the evening before term starts, one of you is online at 11pm trying to find out whether it's a normal start time or an early one, and the other one is ironing things that could have been ironed at any point in the previous two weeks.

The good news is that this specific crisis is entirely preventable. Not all of it — children will always find something unexpected to derail the first morning — but most of it. The term transition gets dramatically easier when you treat it as a project and start it a week before it's due.

## Start the Week Before, Not the Night Before

Give yourself five days, ten minutes each. You need to do a uniform audit — not "probably fine," but actually try things on. Shoes especially. The shoe situation at the start of term is almost always worse than expected. Order replacements now while there's still time.

Then gather all the school communications from last term and read them. Yes, all of them. You're looking for anything that applies to the new term — schedule changes, club registrations, permission slips, payment deadlines. Transfer relevant dates to the shared calendar as you go.

Activities and transport come next. Write down every recurring commitment for the coming term — day, time, location, who needs to bring what. Check for clashes. Confirm anything that needs confirming. Set up recurring reminders for the things that need advance preparation (Wednesday swimming: pack kit Tuesday night).

Sort the first week's meal plan while you're at it. The first week of term is not the week to improvise dinner. Five quick meals decided in advance, shopping list added, one source of evening stress gone.

And then make sure both parents are looking at the same picture. Not a briefing — an actual check that the shared calendar is current, both of you know the week's commitments, both know who's doing what.

## The Activity Schedule Problem

By the end of term, activity schedules have usually evolved in ways that neither parent fully remembers. Swimming moved to a different time. Football has an extra session for a tournament. The Monday club is on Tuesday for the first two weeks because of the hall.

These variations are in a WhatsApp group or an email or someone's voicemail. The only version that's useful is the one that's in your shared calendar, at the right time, on the right days. Take the half hour at the start of term to set this up properly. It saves weeks of uncertainty.

The transport logistics for term are also worth working through explicitly. Which days does each child need to be where, at what time, and who's responsible for the pickup? If carpooling is involved, confirm the arrangements now rather than the morning of. A quick message to the other parents in the pool, with the term dates mapped out, takes ten minutes and prevents a term's worth of last-minute scrambles.

## The Homework Rhythm

Homework doesn't need to be a battleground if the routine is clear from the start of term rather than being negotiated every evening. Where does homework happen? When does it happen? What's the expectation about checking it?

These decisions are easier made at the start of term when nobody is tired and there's no specific homework to argue about. "Homework straight after school, at the kitchen table, then screens" is a policy. Applied consistently from the first week, it becomes the default. Introduced on a Wednesday in week three when you've already had three arguments about it, it's a new rule that feels arbitrary.
