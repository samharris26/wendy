There it is again—the crumpled letter in the book bag. This time, it's announcing another school fundraiser. You glance at the clock. It's nearly 9pm, and you still haven't sorted the dinner leftovers. If only the chore chart on the fridge wasn't just decorative artwork by now. You've attempted everything from reward systems to colourful stickers, yet by week three, they're forgotten. It’s not your fault. Chore charts have a life cycle, and it's often short.

The trick is not in crafting the perfect chart but in creating a system that sustains itself. Here’s a guide on chores for kids by age, and how to actually keep it going.

## Chores for ages 4–6: Start small and simple

At this age, chores are about introducing responsibility, not tackling the washing up. Keep it simple and small:

- Putting toys back in the box post-play
- Matching socks out of the laundry pile
- Watering the plant they picked

Toddlers love feeling grown-up and capable, so tie their chores to tasks they see around them. Instilling a sense of ownership is key. Maybe the plant they water is their special responsibility. Praise, not perfection, is what counts here.

## Chores for ages 7–10: Growing independence

This is the age when you can start expecting a bit more, like folding their own clothes or setting the table:

- Making their own bed (sort of)
- Sorting recycling into the right bins
- Helping with preparing simple snacks

Kids this age are all about peer approval. Link chores to positive outcomes they care about. Want extra screen time? Well, the recycling won't sort itself.

## Chores for ages 11–14: Real teamwork

Now we're talking. These kids can handle real responsibility:

- Taking out the rubbish on bin night
- Cooking a basic meal once a week
- Sweeping or hoovering common areas

This is where your shared family calendar comes into play. Visibility is everything. An easy way for them to see what needs doing keeps the nagging at bay. It's a subtle reminder that's not easily ignored.

## Chores for ages 15+: Preparing for the real world

Teenagers should be gearing up for independent living, so let them:

- Washing their own clothes
- Regularly cleaning their own space
- Taking turns with grocery shopping (with a list)

Tie chores to real-world incentives; think mobile data or money for outings. At this point, they're old enough to see how chores fit into the bigger picture of family life. It's not just about them—it's about everyone.

## Why chore charts die by week three

Here’s the brutal truth: No chore chart survives without being alive to the chaos of family life. It's not about failure; it's about fit. By week three, life is busy and routine kicks in. Things get forgotten if there’s not a system to keep the chart visible and active. This is where rotation, visibility, and personal interest come into play.

### Visibility is key

Chores need to be visible. Enter the digital age. With something like a [shared family calendar](https://www.asknoa.app/features/shared-family-calendar), there’s no hiding from what needs to be done. It’s scheduled, and it’s shared.

### Rotation keeps it fresh

Rotate chores so everyone gets short bursts of different tasks. This keeps it from becoming tedious. If they only ever hoover, it's not helping anyone.

### Link to interests

Chores are way more palatable when tied to something the child cares about. Whether it’s extra time on the PlayStation or earning some pocket money, find out what works as currency in your household.

Keeping chores going is about making tasks part of life that naturally ebb. Let the children play a role in deciding these chores. The more ownership they feel, the more they’ll be inclined to continue. Remember, life with kids isn’t picture-perfect. But with systems that involve the whole family visibly and tangibly, you’ll at least be one step closer to order.
