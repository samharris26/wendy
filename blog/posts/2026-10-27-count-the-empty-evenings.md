It's ten past five on a Tuesday. One child is doing their homework on their knees in a leisure centre car park, because there are forty minutes between gymnastics and swimming and it isn't worth driving home. The other one is in the back of the car eating a cereal bar that is, technically, dinner. Nobody is miserable. But nobody chose this in one go, either. It arrived one sign-up form at a time, each of which looked completely reasonable on its own.

That's the thing about clubs. No single yes is the problem. The problem is that you never sit down and look at all the yeses together.

## Count the evenings, not the clubs

Parents usually ask the question as "how many activities should a child do?" — two, three, one per term? I think that's the wrong unit, because it measures the child and ignores the house. Three clubs for an only child with one parent driving is a gentle week. Two clubs each across three children, spread over four nights, is a shift pattern.

So count evenings instead. Not how many things each person does, but how many nights this household has nothing scheduled after school.

:::checklist{label="The two-minute test"}
- Write Monday to Friday down the side of a page, plus Saturday morning if you have matches
- Mark every slot that has anything in it: club, lesson, training, scouts, music practice with a teacher
- Include the lifts you do for other people's children, because those are evenings too
- Count what's left blank. That's your real number
:::

Do it for the term you're actually in, not the one you remember. Most weeks have quietly gained something since September.

## Two clear evenings is the line I'd hold

One free evening isn't slack. One free evening is where the dentist goes, and the birthday party, and the late meeting, and the night someone comes home with a project due tomorrow that they've known about for three weeks. Fill the other four and your buffer is gone the first time life does something normal.

Two is the number I'd defend. The first absorbs the admin. The second is the one where a child can be bored on the sofa, or a friend can come round without three days' notice, or you can cook something that isn't a cereal bar in a car park.

:::quote
The question isn't how many clubs each child can handle. It's how many the house can handle.
:::

If you're at one clear evening and everything still works, fine — you know your family better than I do. But I'd notice it, rather than discover it in March when everyone is shouting about football socks.

## A one-hour club is never one hour

The club says 4.30 to 5.30. The actual cost is the kit that has to be washed and found, the fifteen minutes there and the fifteen back, the sibling who comes along and does nothing, dinner pushed to 7.45, and the subs that need paying before half-term. None of that appears on the timetable.

It also lands unevenly. In most houses one person is holding the whole list of what needs washing, paying and remembering, and that person is not usually the one who signed the child up. If that's a familiar feeling, I've written about [sharing the mental load](/blog/the-invisible-job-mental-load) separately — but the short version is that every club needs a named adult, not a vague household intention.

## Whose club is this, honestly?

Worth asking before the next sign-up, and worth answering truthfully: who wants this one? Sometimes it's the child. Sometimes it's the parent who wishes they'd stuck at an instrument. Sometimes it's a friendship that's already moved on, and the club is just the last place it lived.

Children are allowed to stop things. I'd rather a child did two things they'd choose again than four they're enduring to avoid a conversation.

## Use the end of term as the exit

The easiest way to drop a club is to never have promised it forever. Say it out loud at the start: we're doing this one for the term, and we'll see in December.

Then the end of term does the work. Nobody quits, nobody gives up, nothing is a defeat — the term simply ends, and you choose again. It also gives you three natural points a year to run the two-minute count, which is roughly how often the week needs reviewing anyway.

## Protect the evening you get back

The risk, once you've cleared a night, is that something fills it within a fortnight. So treat the empty evening as a fixture. Put it in the calendar with a name — "nothing on" works — so it's visible when someone's deciding where the new thing goes.

It pairs well with reading the week out loud on a Sunday, which is the other habit that stops clubs accumulating by accident. I've written the whole twenty-minute version [here](/blog/sunday-reset-routine).

The count isn't a rule, and it isn't a judgement of anyone's week. It's just a way of making a decision you're already making — one form at a time, in the kitchen, on your phone — somewhere you can actually see it. And if a pen and the back of an envelope does it, that's a perfectly good Tuesday's work too.
