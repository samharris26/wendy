## The Productivity App Trap

Somewhere in your phone there are apps you downloaded specifically to get organised. A habit tracker, a shared calendar, a reminder app, a grocery list app, a notes app for the things that don't fit anywhere else, and possibly a task manager that someone on the internet was very enthusiastic about.

Some of these you use. Most of them you open occasionally, find vaguely useful, mean to use more consistently, and then don't. The information you need is split across four different places, none of which is exactly right, and finding something you captured last week requires a small archaeological expedition through notification centres and app folders.

This is app overload, and it's genuinely counterproductive. The friction of having to decide which app something belongs in, the cognitive overhead of maintaining multiple systems, the fact that nothing is in a single place you trust — all of this makes the organisation worse, not better.

## Why More Tools Means Less Organisation

The fundamental problem with having multiple organisational apps is that none of them are reliable. A task that might be in one of three places is, from a practical standpoint, in none of them. You check one, you don't find it, you assume you imagined it. A shared calendar that only one person updates isn't shared — it's one person's calendar that the other person is occasionally briefed about.

The value of a system comes from its consistency. A simple system you actually use beats a sophisticated system you forget to open. When something needs to go somewhere, you need to know immediately which somewhere that is — not to run through a mental list of options and pick the most appropriate.

The goal is a single system where everything that matters to the household lives: calendar events, tasks, shopping lists, reminders. One place. Both partners. Always current.

## What to Look for in a Single Tool

The test is not features. You don't need the app with the most functionality — you need the app both of you will actually open every day without deciding to.

Ease of adding things matters most. If adding a task requires navigating menus, it won't get added in the moments that count — the school run, the checkout, half-asleep at midnight. Voice input or a quick message changes this completely. You can capture things in under ten seconds, which means you'll actually capture them rather than meaning to later.

Shared access is non-negotiable. Both partners need to see the same picture without one of them having to relay information to the other. When something's added, both see it. When something's done, both know. The "I thought you were handling it" conversation is eliminated by the information being visible to both people in real time.

And it needs to be the kind of tool that your partner will actually use — which usually means it has to live somewhere they already are, like WhatsApp, rather than requiring them to adopt an entirely new behaviour.

## The Consolidation Move

If you're currently running multiple apps and none of them feel quite right, the consolidation is worth doing even though it feels like effort. Pick one system. Migrate what matters. Delete the rest.

This works best if it's done together, not as one partner presenting the other with a new system and expecting adoption. Both people deciding on the tool, setting it up together, and committing to it as the household's shared system is the version that actually sticks.

The paradox of family organisation is that the more you try to optimise it, the more complicated it gets. The answer is usually less, not more.
