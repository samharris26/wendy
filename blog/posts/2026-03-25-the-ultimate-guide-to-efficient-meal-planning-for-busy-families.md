## The 6pm Problem

Every weekday evening, somewhere in this country, a tired parent is staring into a fridge with nothing in it and that specific, hollow panic of realising that dinner has to happen in the next forty-five minutes and nobody has thought about what it is.

The irony is that this moment — the 6pm crisis — takes more time and mental energy than any amount of advance planning would have required. You spend twenty minutes deciding what you can make with what you've got, then another twenty minutes actually making it, plus the background anxiety of knowing you'll be having this same crisis tomorrow.

Meal planning is the obvious solution, and most people know this. The reason they don't do it isn't lack of awareness. It's that "meal planning" sounds like something that requires a spreadsheet, a subscription to a recipe app, and energy they don't have. It doesn't. In its most basic form, it takes ten minutes on a Sunday and saves an hour of crisis management every single week.

## The Minimum Viable Meal Plan

Here's the honest version: you don't need five planned dinners, a shopping list organised by supermarket aisle, and a prep schedule. You need to know, very roughly, what you're having each evening this week.

That's it. Not a detailed recipe breakdown. Not a nutritional overview. Just: Monday is pasta, Tuesday is the leftovers from that thing I'm making in bulk on Sunday, Wednesday is something quick because we don't get in until seven, Thursday I might make that chicken thing, Friday is takeaway because it's Friday.

Ten minutes of thinking this through on Sunday has several downstream effects that are worth more than the time invested. You know what you need to buy, so the shopping trip is efficient rather than aspirational. You know what's happening each evening, so you're not having the dinner decision in real time when you're already tired. You're not over-buying things that will quietly expire in the back of the fridge.

And one specific habit that makes a disproportionate difference: knowing which evenings you're home late, and planning something genuinely quick for those nights. A pasta bake that takes forty minutes is wonderful on a Tuesday when you're home at 5pm. It is not wonderful on a Thursday when you walk in at 7pm to hungry children and no emotional reserves. Plan for your actual week, not your aspirational one.

## Batch Cooking as an Act of Self-Care

Batch cooking has an unfortunate reputation as a hobby for extremely organised people, but it's one of the most effective things an exhausted parent can do on a Sunday afternoon. You're cooking anyway. Cooking more of the same thing requires almost no extra effort, and the payoff — a container in the freezer that is a future dinner you don't have to think about — is significant.

It doesn't have to be elaborate. A big pot of soup. A double portion of bolognese. A tray of roasted vegetables that can go with several things. The specific benefit of batch cooking isn't variety or nutrition — it's mental bandwidth. A Thursday when the freezer has something in it is a very different Thursday from one where it doesn't.

The one investment worth making: good containers, labelled with what's in them and when you made them. The mystery container at the back of the freezer that might be soup or might be stew is not the point. Clear labelling means you can see at a glance what you've got, and plan around it.

## The Shared Shopping List

The shopping list is where meal planning meets household reality. A list that lives on your phone that your partner might or might not have access to is not a shared shopping list. It's a personal list that occasionally gets delegated.

A genuinely shared list — where both of you can see it, add to it, and know when things have been crossed off — changes the grocery dynamic meaningfully. Whoever is near a supermarket can pick things up without needing a briefing. Whoever thinks of something can add it without sending a message. And neither of you is buying the same thing because you each thought the other had it handled.

[Noa](https://apps.apple.com/gb/app/noa-your-life-organised/id6760316752) is useful here because the shopping list is shared by default and you can add to it by voice — which means when you notice you've run out of something while making the school lunches at 7am, you say it out loud and it goes on the list, without stopping what you're doing.

The dinner crisis is preventable. It just requires taking the problem seriously on Sunday when you still have options, rather than on Wednesday when you don't.
