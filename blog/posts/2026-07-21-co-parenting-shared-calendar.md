Handover day is when most co-parenting friction shows up. Not because anyone's being difficult, but because the plan lived in a text thread from three weeks ago, and one of you read "the 14th" as the Thursday and the other as the Friday.

A shared calendar won't fix a difficult relationship. What it can do is take the logistics out of the conversation, so the conversations you do have are about the kids and not about whose weekend it was.

## One calendar, not two

The most common setup is each parent keeping their own calendar and messaging the other when something changes. It means there are always two versions of the week, and they drift.

One shared calendar for the children, which both of you can see and edit, removes the drift. Your own work diary stays separate. The kids' calendar is the single source of truth for where they are and who's got them.

## What goes in it

Keep it to the things both homes need to know:

:::checklist{label="The shared calendar holds"}
- Who has the children, every day, as an all-day entry
- Handover times and places
- School events, clubs, parties and appointments
- Anything that needs to travel between houses: PE kit, a costume, medicine
:::

Things that don't belong: your own social plans, reminders about each other, anything you wouldn't want the other parent to read.

## Agree who changes what

Most shared-calendar rows come down to one thing: someone changed an entry without saying. Two simple rules prevent almost all of it.

First, whoever has the children that day owns the entries for that day. They can add or move things freely.

Second, anything that changes the other parent's time (swapping a weekend, moving a handover) is asked about first and only goes in the calendar once it's agreed. The calendar records decisions. It isn't where you make them.

:::quote
The calendar isn't there to prove who was right. It's there so nobody has to ask.
:::

## Put the kit in the calendar too

A surprising number of handover arguments are about things, not times. The swimming bag stayed at Dad's, the reading book is at Mum's. Adding a line to the entry ("take PE kit and the blue folder") costs nothing and saves a trip across town.

## Let the kids see it, when they're old enough

Older children often feel happier when they can see the plan themselves. It stops them being the messenger ("Mum says can you…"), which is a job no child should have. A read-only view on their own phone is usually enough.

## The quiet benefit

After a few months, the calendar becomes a record of how things actually went: who did which pickups, which weekends swapped. You'll probably never need it for anything formal. But it's there, calmly, without anyone having to keep score.
